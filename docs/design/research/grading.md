# Grading research for pi-arena

Scope: letter grades, composites, ranks, user weighting, neutral wording, tone. Every URL below was opened this session unless marked otherwise. Claims I could not verify are tagged [INFERENCE] or [SNIPPET ONLY] (seen in a search result, page not readable).

## Recommendations for pi-arena

**R1. Do not show letters. Anchor on a neutral reference line instead.**
- Letter cards do move behaviour, but they also create cliffs. After Los Angeles posted A/B/C cards, inspection scores spiked at 90 (the A cutoff) and dipped at 89, which the authors read as inspectors bumping borderline scores ([Jin & Leslie, Choices 2005](https://econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/jin-leslie-choices-final.pdf)). In Florida, schools just over a B/C cutoff were rated 0.36 to 0.57 of a letter higher by residents than near-identical schools just under it ([Chingos et al., Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/grading-schools)). House-price effects came from "arbitrary distinctions embedded in school report cards" ([Figlio & Lucas, NBER w8019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8019)).
- Letters read as verdicts, so the graded party defends itself. Yale, Harvard and Berkeley law schools quit U.S. News, calling the formula "profoundly flawed" ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2022/nov/17/yale-harvard-law-school-us-news-world-report-rankings)). Goldstein and Spiegelhalter ask for comparison "in a spirit of collaboration rather than confrontation" ([paper](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/cmm/migrated/documents/limitations-of-league-tables.pdf)). That is your owner's instinct, with a citation.
- Letters help when a regulator needs lay diners to act. Jin & Leslie say the letter had "a clear interpretation to consumers", and their own suggestion is to print the numeric score beside it. pi-arena readers are developers who can read a ratio, so the lay-audience benefit does not apply. The causal claim itself is contested: [Ho et al. (SIEPR)](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/false-promise-simple-information-disclosure-new-evidence-restaurant) found the 20% hospitalisation drop does not survive placebo tests.
- Arithmetic on your formula (my derivation): 10 points = one halving of cost, so A (90) needs 0.25x Pi's cost and B (80) needs 0.5x. A harness 5% leaner than Pi scores about 70.7, 30% leaner about 75.1. Real differences would pile up around "C" and the letter boundaries would sit inside the noise floor, which is the cliff pattern above.
- What letters were buying is "evaluability": a good/bad cue that tells readers whether a number is large. Stars-only raised choice of the highest-quality plan from 49% to 73% vs bars alone ([Hibbard et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1430368)). You can get that cue without a verdict: draw Pi = 1.00x as a vertical reference line, shade each harness's A/A noise band, and print "about the same as Pi" when the interval straddles 1.00. Benchstat's `~` (no significant difference) is the same idea ([benchstat output](https://www.bwplotka.dev/2024/go-microbenchmarks-benchstat/)).

**R2. Show a composite, but as a cost ratio, not points, and keep it beside its chart.**
- Single composites lose information for most items. The PACE study of California schools found only 40% of schools in the bottom 5% on academic performance were flagged by an equal-weight summative score ([Hough & Kirst, Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/californias-dashboard-data-will-guide-improvement-forum-hough-kirst-accountability/)). So the per-family ratios stay primary and the composite is a one-line summary, not the headline.
- Summaries also shift how people weight things without their noticing: 35% of 107 participants changed preference when the same measures were summarized vs split, yet their self-reported weights did not move ([Hibbard et al., Exp. IV](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1430368)). That argues for printing the weights next to the composite every time.
- Use the geometric mean of ratios: Fleming & Wallace show that the arithmetic mean of normalized benchmark results "leads to mistaken conclusions" that the geometric mean avoids ([1986 abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/5666.5673)). Benchstat prints a `geomean` row per metric ([blog showing real output](https://www.bwplotka.dev/2024/go-microbenchmarks-benchstat/)); js-framework-benchmark moved to a weighted geometric mean from Chrome 118 ([README](https://github.com/krausest/js-framework-benchmark), [wiki](https://github.com/krausest/js-framework-benchmark/wiki/Computation-of-the-weighted-geometric-mean)); ClickBench scores by geometric mean of ratios ([QuestDB write-up](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)).
- Your formula already is one: without clamping, the overall score equals 70 - 10*log2(weighted geomean of family ratios) (my derivation). The 70 anchor, the factor 10 and the 0..100 clamp add a second scale and a floor/ceiling (ratios of 128x or more and 0.125x or less saturate) that buy nothing for readers. Publish the weighted geomean ratio ("0.83x Pi, 95% 0.78 to 0.89") as the composite. If a points scale is kept, drop the clamp.
- Keep Pi as a fixed reference, not "fastest per query". Rankings against a moving best change when others change: in QuestDB's rerun Polars did identical work yet fell from 1.361 to 1.485 and lost its near-tie for first, "The baseline moved underneath it". A fixed Pi baseline avoids that.

**R3. Treat ranks as ranges, and compute them from the bootstrap, not from CI overlap.**
- Goldstein & Spiegelhalter: ranks are the most sampling-sensitive output, intervals on ranks are easy to simulate, and with them "finely graded comparisons among institutions are impossible"; rankings are fine for screening, not for definitive judgement. In their A-level example about two thirds of pairwise comparisons could not be separated; in the NY surgeon data 46% of surgeons moved between halves of the list in a year (as reported there).
- Spiegelhalter's funnel plot paper recommends plotting estimate vs precision with control limits and says it "avoids spurious ranking of institutions into league tables" ([Stat Med 2005 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15568194)). For you, interval plots with the Pi line and the noise band do the same job.
- Rank range per harness = the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of that harness's rank across bootstrap draws (Section 3.2 of the 1996 paper does this). Caution: two standard 95% intervals that overlap do not prove "cannot be separated"; Goldstein and Healy use rescaled overlap intervals for pairwise claims ([same paper](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/cmm/migrated/documents/limitations-of-league-tables.pdf)). If you say "ranks overlap", derive it from the rank draws.
- Numeric ranges do not cost you trust. In 5 experiments (n = 5,780), numeric ranges with a point estimate did not significantly lower trust in numbers or in the source, while verbal hedges ("could be higher or lower") did ([van der Bles et al., PNAS 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149229)). Show "0.83x (0.78 to 0.89)", not "roughly".

**R4. Let users grade with presets first, plus a "your mix" panel that is honest about uncertainty.**
- Presets are published, named, with intervals and rank ranges. They also act as the default. [SNIPPET ONLY] Defaults are sticky: Johnson & Goldstein reported 82% vs 42% organ-donor enrolment between opt-out and opt-in framings (quote from a ResearchGate snippet; Science page returned 403). So choose the default preset deliberately and say why.
- Sliders alone are risky. Users cannot hold weights steady: after summarize/split changes in Hibbard Exp. IV they "had difficulty weighting various quality factors consistently" and did not notice ([Hibbard et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1430368)). OECD's own BLI paper warns users "may not directly realise" that a 0 to 5 rating is read as relative to the other sliders ([OECD WP 2018/03](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/07/what-matters-the-most-to-people_740a2959/edf9a89a-en.pdf)). So make the control a set of percent shares that sum to 100 (Hibbard used a 100-point split) rather than independent 0 to 5 knobs.
- Free weights can crown nearly anyone. For the 2014 BLI, 19 of 36 countries can reach #1 under some weight vector, often by zeroing most dimensions ([Lorenz et al., arXiv 1608.04556](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.04556)). Enforce a minimum share per family (they suggest a minimum weight of one) or at least show a "zeroed families" warning.
- Weight labels do not equal influence when criteria correlate: stated weights and measured importance often differ strongly in HDI and university league tables ([Paruolo, Saisana & Saltelli 2013](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC64676)). CPU time and instructions will be correlated here [INFERENCE]; consider showing "effective influence" or merging near-duplicate metrics within a family.
- Interval for custom weights: because the composite is linear in the log-ratios, a custom-weight interval can be recomputed in the browser from stored bootstrap draws of the family log-ratios, if the draws are joint (same resample index across families) and shipped (rows = draws, columns = family, per harness) [INFERENCE about your pipeline]. That removes the "only presets have an interval" problem. If you do not ship draws, show custom mixes as point estimates only, label them "no interval for custom mixes", and add a sensitivity strip: each harness's min to max overall across the five presets. [WeightLifter](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875176) reports that showing sensitivity to weight changes raised "awareness of uncertainty in the ultimate decisions".
- Precedent for the interaction itself: the BLI has 130,000 users who shared weights, shows a flower per country, and lets you compare with others ([OECD WP 2018/03](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/07/what-matters-the-most-to-people_740a2959/edf9a89a-en.pdf)). A URL that encodes the weights makes custom views shareable and reproducible [INFERENCE].
- Say which task suite you chose. Swapping tasks can change who looks best ([Benchmark Lottery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07002)), and a one-fair-change tweak reshuffled ClickBench rankings ([QuestDB](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)). One line: "These numbers are for these N scenarios and this mock model."

**R5. Word comparisons as measurements, not outcomes.**
- Use "uses 0.83x of Pi's CPU time" or "1.2x Pi's peak memory", always "vs Pi". Avoid wins, beats, loses, worse, grade, fails. Use benchstat's grammar: "vs base", a `~` for no clear difference, and the interval in parentheses ([benchstat output](https://www.bwplotka.dev/2024/go-microbenchmarks-benchstat/)). Call the composite "relative cost index" (lower = less overhead).
- Pi's 1.00x has no interval by definition; say "Pi is the ruler" so absence of an interval is explained rather than looking like a hole [INFERENCE].
- State direction per metric in plain words ("less memory") since lower-is-better is not obvious on a mixed chart [INFERENCE].
- Gaming hygiene, because the mock model is scripted and public: publish every run with no retraction, cap resubmissions, same run count for every harness, and keep the scenario list versioned. The Leaderboard Illusion found best-of-N private variants (27 from Meta) inflate scores, unequal sampling, and silent deprecations ([arXiv 2504.20879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879), [full text](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.20879)). Hold out some scenarios if you want to detect special-casing [INFERENCE].

**R6. Tone: self-deprecating about the benchmark, generous to the baseline.**
- Closest examples found are about disclaiming the benchmark, not teasing the reference. The Benchmarks Game: "We are profoundly uninterested in claims that these measurements ... define ... 'Which programming language is fastest'" and "Anyone else a bit shocked by how well Javascript on V8 performs? I might need to rethink my assumptions" ([Why toy programs?](https://benchmarksgame-team.pages.debian.net/benchmarksgame/why-measure-toy-benchmark-programs.html)). QuestDB's post imagines a race where athletes must whistle "Yellow Submarine" while running, "the quickest sprinter on the track can easily finish last" ([post](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)).
- I did not find a page that jokes about its own baseline. Draft lines for the owner to judge (mine, not sourced): "Pi is the ruler. Everyone else gets measured against it, and the ruler has never once been late." / "Differences under the noise band get a shrug (~), not a trophy."

## Evidence

### 1. Letter grades and composites versus raw measures
- **Restaurant hygiene cards, LA.** Jin & Leslie (QJE 2003): after the 1998 mandate, A revenue rose 5.7%, B 0.7%, C fell 1%; scores bunched at the 90 cutoff; before cards, revenue was unrelated to inspection scores ([Choices summary](https://econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/jin-leslie-choices-final.pdf)). They argue between-restaurant spread (13.5 points) exceeds within-restaurant spread (8.8), so the grade is "informative, although imperfect." Later work disputes causality: placebo tests show equal drops in nearby counties not changing grading ([Ho et al., SIEPR WP](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/false-promise-simple-information-disclosure-new-evidence-restaurant)). Takeaway: cliffs and gaming around cutoffs are robust; the health effect is not settled.
- **School A-F.** Residents' ratings track proficiency, and Florida B/C cutoffs add a jump unrelated to quality ([Chingos et al.](https://www.educationnext.org/grading-schools)). Critics: single summative scores are "arbitrary" for all but the worst 1% of schools, and California's old API was nicknamed the "Affluent Parent Index" ([Hough & Kirst](https://www.educationnext.org/californias-dashboard-data-will-guide-improvement-forum-hough-kirst-accountability/)). That piece is an advocate for dashboards; the other side of the forum (Jeb Bush, "intuitive letter grades") was linked but I did not read it.
- **Hospital stars.** CMS's overall rating is a weighted mean of five measure-group scores (22/22/22/22/12%) clustered by k-means into 1 to 5 stars. Adding a peer-grouping step changed the stars for 585 of 3,076 hospitals (19.0%), 517 up and 68 down ([Gettel et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11099678)). Method choice moves categories. JAMA's critique is titled "The Stars Are Not Aligned" ([Bilimoria & Barnard 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27802552), abstract not available; AAMC rebuttal returned an access wall).
- **Evaluability.** Hibbard et al. use Hsee's principle: attributes get weight in proportion to how easily they evoke a good/bad feeling, "outside of the decision maker's conscious awareness." Stars and ordering raised use of quality data; summarizing biased weights ([PMC1430368](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1430368)). They also say graded standards (unacceptable/acceptable/excellent) would be more evaluable but need expert consensus. Review: [Hibbard & Peters 2003](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Supporting-informed-consumer-health-care-decisions%3A-Hibbard-Peters/ced9266476e91d8ca5727819809a40378c86dc0b) [SNIPPET ONLY] "less is more".
- **Reactivity.** Espeland & Sauder: public rankings change behaviour via self-fulfilling prophecy and commensuration ([AJS 2007 abstract](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/rankings-and-reactivity-how-public-measures-recreate-social-world)). Columbia's U.S. News rank fell from 2nd to 18th after a professor showed misreported data ([Thaddeus](https://www.math.columbia.edu/~thaddeus/ranking/investigation.html), [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2022/nov/17/yale-harvard-law-school-us-news-world-report-rankings)).
- **Not verified:** Consumer Reports/Wirecutter methods (CR search listings suggest weights favour tested performance over reliability and that rating tiers have cutpoints; the PDFs returned 403, so [SNIPPET ONLY]); credit-score indices (not researched); studies of defensiveness of graded parties beyond the law-school and restaurant cases.

### 2. Leaderboards, ranks, uncertainty
- **League tables.** Goldstein & Spiegelhalter (JRSS A 1996): uncertainty intervals on estimates and ranks; adjusted tables inherit unadjusted problems; "regression to the mean" explains apparent improvement at the bottom; tables need "an accompanying health warning"; gaming examples (reported CHF risk factor rising from 1.7% to 7.6% after a NY programme) ([paper](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/cmm/migrated/documents/limitations-of-league-tables.pdf)).
- **Funnel plots.** Spiegelhalter 2005: estimate vs precision with control limits, avoiding ranks ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15568194)).
- **Leaderboard Illusion.** Private best-of-N testing with score retraction biases the arena rating upward; 27 Meta variants in one month; proprietary providers got more battles; 205 of 243 models silently deprecated; "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it" (Goodhart) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.20879)). The authors' fixes (no retraction, limits on variants, equal removal, fair sampling, deprecation transparency) are cheap for a static dashboard to copy.
- **Benchmark Lottery.** Relative performance of methods "may be altered significantly simply by choosing different benchmark tasks" ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07002)).
- **Moving baselines.** QuestDB's ClickBench rerun: one fair change (keep processes alive) re-ranked engines; Hyper went from last to third; Polars worsened with identical work ([post](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)). Vendor post, but the arithmetic is checkable.
- **Uncertainty communication.** See R3 ([van der Bles et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149229)). Caveat: UK news-style numbers, general public, not developers.
- **Rank vs score perception** (do people over-read ranks?): no direct experiment found; evidence here is indirect (Goldstein et al.; Chingos jump at cutoffs).

### 3. User-defined weights and personalised rankings
- **OECD BLI.** Users set 0 to 5 on 11 dimensions; OECD itself keeps a dashboard for its flagship How's Life? partly because aggregation involves "potentially controversial choices". The tool was built "to involve people in discussions on well-being." Health, life satisfaction and education get the highest average weights; users are self-selected and not representative; the application cannot identify unique users ([WP 2018/03](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/07/what-matters-the-most-to-people_740a2959/edf9a89a-en.pdf)). Gaming by weights: 19 of 36 countries can be made #1 ([Lorenz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.04556)).
- **Weights vs importance.** [Paruolo et al.](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC64676): declared importance and measured "main effect" differ a lot, and correlation structure may prevent obtaining the stated importance.
- **U.S. News DIY.** Rankings depend on self-reported data and fixed weights; withdrawals followed. I did not find usability data on DIY rankers; the closest is [LineUp](https://sci.utah.edu/~vdl/papers/2013_infovis_lineup.pdf) (multi-attribute rankings with user weights; paper not read in full) and [WeightLifter](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875176) (weight-space exploration up to ten criteria; qualitative feedback only).
- **Sliders.** "Exploring the Design Space of Three Criteria Decision Making" (IEEE CG&A 2023) compares slider designs for 3 criteria; page returned 403, only the search snippet was seen [SNIPPET ONLY]. I found no controlled study comparing presets against free sliders for weighting. Reasoning above rests on Hibbard (inconsistent weighting) and BLI/Lorenz (misuse), so the "presets primary, custom secondary" call is [INFERENCE] supported by those pieces.

### 4. Composites without judgement words
- **Geometric mean.** Fleming & Wallace's rule: do not average normalized numbers arithmetically; use the geometric mean ([ACM abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/5666.5673); the UNSW PDF copy extracted incompletely, so I relied on the abstract).
- **Benchstat.** Output is a table with `sec/op`, `vs base` percentages, `± noise`, `(p=… n=…)`, `~` when no difference, and a `geomean` row per metric; no overall verdict ([example](https://www.bwplotka.dev/2024/go-microbenchmarks-benchstat/)). I could not open the official benchstat docs (pkg.go.dev returned an empty page), so details come from this third-party blog.
- **js-framework-benchmark.** Slowdown factor vs fastest, weighted geometric mean, with weights from the 90th percentile of factors so a lone outlier does not dominate ([wiki](https://github.com/krausest/js-framework-benchmark/wiki/Computation-of-the-weighted-geometric-mean)). Another published, adjustable weight scheme, though weights are derived from spread, not user priorities.
- **ClickBench.** Ratio vs fastest per query with a 10 ms cushion, then geometric mean ([QuestDB](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)).

### 5. Tone examples in dev tooling
- Benchmarks Game: openly disclaims "which language is fastest" and describes its own programs as toy ([front page](https://benchmarksgame-team.pages.debian.net/benchmarksgame/index.html), [Why toy programs?](https://benchmarksgame-team.pages.debian.net/benchmarksgame/why-measure-toy-benchmark-programs.html)).
- QuestDB's "Lies, Damn Lies" post: whimsical analogy, then a precise reproducible experiment ([post](https://questdb.com/blog/lies-damn-lies-and-database-benchmarks)).
- Pattern worth copying [INFERENCE]: jokes go at the benchmark's own limits, numbers stay plain. No example found where the baseline is teased.

## Gaps
No source tested letters vs ratios on developer audiences. No controlled preset-vs-slider study located. Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, credit-score claims are unverified. Official benchstat documentation and the 2023 slider-design paper were not readable.
